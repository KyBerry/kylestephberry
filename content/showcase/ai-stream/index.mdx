I built this on a weekend after noticing that most AI chat interfaces feel mechanical even when
the model is fast. The text lurches forward in uneven jumps — a burst of five words, a pause,
another burst — and the experience reads as network instability rather than thinking. I wanted
to understand whether a small scheduling layer could make the same content feel significantly
more natural.

The core problem is that tokens don't arrive at even intervals. A language model emits them in
small bursts with variable gaps between sentences and at punctuation boundaries. Naively rendering
each chunk the moment it arrives preserves all that variance. The fix is to pull each token off the
wire and schedule its appearance with a per-token delay drawn from a small distribution —
roughly 30–80 ms base, with an extra 150–220 ms pause after sentence-ending punctuation. That
smoothing makes the visual rhythm read as deliberate speech rather than network jitter.

The blinking cursor is a deliberate state signal. It appears the moment streaming begins and is
removed as soon as the final token is committed — not on a fixed timer, but in direct response to
completion. I spent more time on this than I expected: a cursor that keeps blinking after the
response finishes reads as "still loading," which creates real anxiety. Syncing blink lifetime
exactly to stream lifetime turned out to matter.

The demo simulates jitter client-side rather than pulling from a live SSE endpoint. Real production
streaming has genuine variance driven by model inference speed, token length, and network RTT —
the simulation approximates the shape of that distribution but can't reproduce occasional
multi-second stalls on hard reasoning steps. For a component library context the simulation is the
right call: it runs offline and keeps the demo deterministic.

Accessibility: the output container uses `aria-live="polite"`, which lets screen readers announce
completed sentences without interrupting mid-stream.
