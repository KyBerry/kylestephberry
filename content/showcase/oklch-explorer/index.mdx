Every color scale on this site is written in OKLCH, and this little tool is why. In OKLCH,
the lightness channel actually means lightness: two colors with the same L read as equally
bright regardless of hue. Build a ten-step ramp in HSL and the yellow steps glow while the
blue steps go muddy. Build the same ramp in OKLCH and the steps stay evenly spaced, which is
the entire job of a ramp.

The explorer holds chroma and hue fixed and walks lightness from 0.25 to 0.96, which is how I
actually construct scales: pick the hue, pick how saturated the family should be, and let
lightness do the stepping. Drag the hue slider across its full range and watch the ramp keep
its visual rhythm. That consistency is the property you are buying with OKLCH.

The contrast readout is the practical half. Each step converts to sRGB by hand (the OKLab
matrices are about fifteen lines of code) and computes a WCAG relative luminance from the
linear values, then reports the ratio against whichever text color the swatch itself chose,
white or black. The tier labels follow the spec: 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text,
7:1 for AAA. Watching the AA boundary move as you drag chroma teaches you more about contrast
than any table of hex codes did for me.

One honest wrinkle: OKLCH can describe colors that sRGB cannot display. Push chroma high and
some steps leave the gamut, at which point this component clamps the channels and labels the
step "clipped." Real CSS engines do something smarter than clamping (gamut mapping toward
lower chroma), but I wanted the failure visible rather than silently prettified, because a
clipped step means your token will not render as the math suggests on most displays today.

The copy interaction mirrors the token explorer elsewhere in this catalog: click a step,
get the `oklch()` string on your clipboard, paste it straight into a CSS custom property.
