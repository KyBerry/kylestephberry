Skeletons have a failure mode nobody designs for: the fast response. When data arrives in
80ms, a naive implementation flashes the skeleton for a single frame and then slams content
into place. It reads as a glitch. The slow path gets all the design attention, and the fast
path (which is most loads, most days) gets the worst experience.

The fix here is a minimum display time. Once the skeleton is visible, it stays visible for at
least 450ms even if the data came back sooner. If the fetch is slower than that, content swaps
in the moment it lands, so the floor never adds latency to a genuinely slow response. Hit
reload a few times and watch the readout: the simulated latency is randomized, so sometimes
the skeleton is held and sometimes it isn't.

The second half of the pattern is layout stability. The skeleton and the content render into
the same grid cell, and the skeleton's line widths are drawn to roughly match the content it
stands in for. The crossfade is a plain opacity transition between two layers that already
occupy identical space, so nothing reflows during the swap. If your skeleton is a different
height than your content, the crossfade just relocates the jank instead of removing it.

Reduced motion gets two accommodations: the shimmer animation is only applied inside a
`prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference` block, and the crossfade collapses to an instant
swap. A shimmering placeholder is exactly the kind of ambient movement that setting exists to
turn off.

One number worth stealing: 450ms. Below roughly 400ms a held skeleton starts to feel like
added latency; much above 500ms and fast responses feel throttled. I landed on 450 by feel
after trying a range, and I'd tune it per surface rather than treat it as a constant.
